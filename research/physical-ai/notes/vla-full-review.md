# Full review: GR00T N1 and OpenVLA

This review supersedes the earlier partial readings of these two archived papers. Every extracted text line was read sequentially, including appendices, references and acknowledgments: GR00T N1 lines 1–2148; OpenVLA lines 1–2173. Line numbers use LF boundaries; each final unterminated formfeed-only line was inspected separately during the coverage audit and is included. All numbered figures and tables were also inspected in rendered PDF pages. Exact hashes, bounded reading ranges and inspected page numbers are in [the coverage record](../reading-vla-full.json). These are literature findings and design deductions; no weights, datasets, inference service or physical robot were run.

Sources are the acquired **GR00T N1, arXiv 2503.14734v2** ([primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14734v2), [archived PDF](../raw/gr00t-n1.pdf)) and **OpenVLA, arXiv 2406.09246v3** ([primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09246v3), [archived PDF](../raw/openvla.pdf)). Section, figure and table references below refer to those exact versions. This is not a review of every later model bearing either name. The separately acquired N1.6 report and newer repository snapshot must retain their own version and evidence records.

## GR00T N1: what is actually specified

Sections 2 and 3 and Figures 2–3 describe a 2.2-billion-parameter system: an Eagle-2 vision-language backbone, with 1.34 billion parameters, supplies features to a flow-matching diffusion transformer. Embodiment-specific state/action encoders and action decoders handle different vector meanings and dimensions. The evaluated setup predicts 16 actions, with four denoising steps. The 224-pixel image encoder yields 64 tokens; the policy uses representations from layer 12 of the language backbone. The frozen language component is explicit in Figure 3, Section 2.3 and Table 6. Calling the architecture jointly trained does not mean every language-model weight is updated, or that its “System 2” is an independently operating task planner.

Appendix E is particularly relevant to an interface standard. It extends LeRobot with a modality description that assigns semantics, ranges, types and rotation representations to vector fields. Its standardization uses 6D end-effector rotation state, axis-angle rotation actions, min-max scaling and a specified ordering across arms. Those choices are model-data conventions, not universal robot conventions. HMS must pin the exact modality description and transforms for each deployed embodiment; equal tensor dimensions do not establish compatibility. Normalization ranges do not replace commissioned physical limits.

The paper reports 63.9 ms to sample a 16-action chunk on an L40 in bf16. The introduction separately describes 10 Hz vision-language processing and 120 Hz actions. Those are different quantities. They neither establish a 120 Hz fresh-observation loop nor bound total latency through exposure, preprocessing, network transfer, scheduling and controller execution. No worst-case or high-percentile deployment latency is supplied. These values cannot be transplanted to GB10, a shared hub or a different precision configuration.

The data pyramid contains unlike evidence. Human videos receive learned latent actions, and generated videos receive latent or inverse-dynamics pseudo-actions. Those labels are inferred training targets, not instrumented motor execution. Table 7 totals about 8,376 hours across real robot, human, simulation and generated-video data; it is misleading to describe that total as physical humanoid teleoperation. The in-house GR-1 data is 88.4 hours and generated neural video is 827.3 hours. The 540,000 simulation trajectories in pretraining and 780,000 including post-training are different scopes. Figure 13's generated liquids and multi-round examples are qualitative; Section 2.3 leaves their downstream quantitative evaluation to future work.

Resource claims also have distinct scopes. Pretraining used roughly 50,000 H100 GPU-hours, while neural-video generation required roughly 105,000 L40 GPU-hours. A single-A6000 fine-tuning experiment accommodates a much smaller batch when the vision encoder is trained than when only adapters and the diffusion transformer are trained. Neither pretraining scale nor a successful fine-tuning configuration proves inference deadlines on an edge appliance.

## GR00T N1: strength and limits of the evaluation

Simulation covers 24 RoboCasa tasks, nine DexMimicGen tasks and 24 GR-1 tabletop tasks, with 30/100/300 demonstrations per task. Section 4.3 reports 100 trials and the maximum score over the last five checkpoints, saved every 500 steps. This is a checkpoint-selection protocol, not a single untouched final-checkpoint estimate. Its selection procedure must accompany comparisons.

There is an unresolved numerical inconsistency in the source, verified against rendered pages rather than inferred from text extraction:

| DexMimicGen, 100 demonstrations | Diffusion Policy | GR00T N1 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Table 2, PDF page 15 | 56.1% | 66.5% |
| Table 4 and Figure 10, PDF pages 26 and 20 | 46.9% | 58.5% |

The paper does not explain this difference. Do not select the more favorable pair or silently treat the two as equivalent. Other detailed results also qualify broad statements about consistent improvement. The GR-1 average is 50.0% at 100 demonstrations and 49.3% at 300; the DexMimicGen box-cleanup task at 100 demonstrations favors the baseline substantially. Aggregate improvement does not imply monotonic learning or superiority on every task.

The real-robot post-training tasks comprise five pick-and-place tasks with seen/unseen variants, three articulated-object tasks, three industrial-object tasks and two coordination tasks. Most use ten trials. Machinery packing uses five trials, with credit for the fraction of five objects packed within 30 seconds. Other tasks also award partial credit. Consequently the 76.8% full-data aggregate in Tables 3/5 is an average task score, not a 76.8% probability of independently completing an arbitrary industrial operation. The two pretrained-policy tasks use only 15 trials each, spanning five objects. They demonstrate transfer within their stated scenes, not broad operational generalization.

Section 4.5 gives a useful failure example: the pretrained model transfers an apple between hands when it is out of right-hand reach, while the post-trained model loses that behavior after training only on right-hand trajectories. Figure 11 illustrates the pretrained transfer. A new model revision therefore cannot inherit every previously permitted skill merely because its average benchmark score improves. Figure 12's caption also mismatches the top/bottom object/task labels visible in its panels; use it as qualitative illustration, not a precise task-level comparison.

The stated limitations include short-horizon tabletop work and incomplete coverage of long-horizon loco-manipulation. Successful demonstrations and synthetic success filtering do not by themselves teach a deployment what to do after lost state, failed communication, manual motion or an emergency stop. Independent lifecycle and termination semantics remain necessary.

## OpenVLA: architecture and deployment specificity

Sections 3–4 and Figure 2 describe a 7B Llama-2 model with fused DINOv2/SigLIP visual features, trained on 970,000 robot trajectories. The evaluated policy consumes an image and instruction and produces a seven-dimensional end-effector/gripper action. It does not have the richer observation history, proprioception and action chunking used by the full Diffusion Policy baseline. The selected training datasets emphasize single-arm manipulation with third-person images and end-effector control; this is not an arbitrary-device action space.

Continuous action dimensions become 256 discrete bins between dataset-specific first and 99th quantiles. The action tokenizer repurposes 256 low-use language tokens. The decoder, quantile statistics, instruction template and image preprocessing are therefore part of the controller's meaning, not interchangeable model-serving options. Quantile bounds ignore outliers for numerical resolution; they are not physical travel limits. The resulting precision must be measured against a deployment's needed accuracy before using the policy for fine positioning.

Training-mixture provenance also matters: DROID initially contributes 10%, then is removed in the last third of training because learning progress is slow. A claim based only on the final mixture table would miss that schedule. Appendix C identifies another consequential preprocessing change: removing first-step zero actions in BridgeData alleviates freezing. The RT-2-X comparison uses a second-most-likely-action workaround; dynamically trying a second prediction after a zero action performs worse as latency changes the dynamics. This supports recording preprocessing and decoding strategy. It does not justify globally discarding zero commands: a physical hold or pause can be intentional.

## OpenVLA: evaluation and hardware caveats

The direct evaluations contain 170 WidowX rollouts per method (17 tasks × 10) and 60 Google-robot rollouts (12 × 5), with matched initial conditions. WidowX permits partial credit for outcomes such as reaching toward or contacting the correct object; Google-robot scoring is binary. The abstract's 29-task improvement combines those evaluation scopes and is not a common probability of completed physical work. RT-2-X remains stronger on the semantic-generalization category in the WidowX comparison. Differences in training data, preprocessing and encoders also prevent attributing the comparison solely to model size.

Adaptation uses seven Franka tasks and 10–150 demonstrations per task, with 99 tabletop and 30 DROID rollouts per method. Tabletop and DROID use 5 Hz and 15 Hz nonblocking controllers. Partial scoring remains present. The full Diffusion Policy baseline has observation history, proprioception, action chunks and absolute Cartesian actions; a separate matched baseline removes those advantages and predicts a single relative action. Figure 5/Table 7 show the full baseline's strength on narrow tasks and OpenVLA's stronger aggregate across diverse language-conditioned tasks. They do not establish OpenVLA as universally smoother or more accurate.

**Footnote 4 materially narrows the compute claims:** Sections 5.3–5.4 use a smaller training mixture and a SigLIP-only variant, rather than the main fused-encoder model. Its LoRA experiment uses 33 rollouts over two representative tasks. Rank 32 trains 1.4% of parameters but still reports 59.7 GB GPU memory at batch 16; the paper's 10–15-hour single-A100 adaptation is not proof of that workload fitting every consumer GPU. Main-model training used 64 A100s for 14 days; Section 3.5 reports approximately 15 GB bf16 inference and approximately 6 Hz on a 4090. Table 2's 16.8 GB bf16, 10.2 GB int8 and 7.0 GB int4 belong to the narrower study. Keep those scopes separate.

The quantization experiment is a direct warning against treating model accuracy and control timing independently. Across eight Bridge tasks and 80 rollouts per precision, Table 2 gives 71.3% bf16, 58.1% int8 and 71.9% int4. On the evaluation A5000, int8 reaches about 1.2 Hz and int4 about 3 Hz, versus the data's 5 Hz nonblocking controller. Appendix D.4 repeats evaluation with blocking control—each action completes before the next is predicted—and finds comparable scores with overlapping error bars: 70.0%, 74.4% and 68.8%, respectively. The evidence supports an effect of changed timing in this setup. It does not establish blocking control as universally preferable, particularly when the world continues changing during inference.

LIBERO is more extensive but remains simulation: four suites of ten tasks, independently trained policies per suite, 500 trials and three seeds per reported suite statistic. Training demonstrations were replayed at a different image resolution, no-op actions removed, images rotated 180 degrees for the authors' rendering setup, and failed replay trajectories removed (68/500 Spatial, 46/500 Object, 72/500 Goal and 121/500 Long). All compared methods use the same curated data and original test initial configurations. The average 76.5% versus 75.1% for Octo and 72.4% for Diffusion Policy must retain that curation context; the 53.7% Long result does not demonstrate dependable real-world long-horizon autonomy.

## Consequences for HMS

The original synthesis is in [the learned-control profile](../../../docs/learned-control-profile.md). The main consequence is that compatibility belongs to a complete deployment: model revision, embodiment mapping, perception/calibration, action decoder, controller mode, scheduling and measured task envelope. A model service returns a proposal. The commissioned executor still owns physical admission, bounds, authority, deadlines, interruption, observed completion and uncertain outcomes.

The papers do not establish HMS conformance, functional safety, CPU viability for their full models, GB10 performance, unknown-device operation, or recovery from HMS fault scenarios. They provide concrete architectural evidence and useful failure cases. Next research should separately evaluate newer asynchronous/chunked policies and controller integrations, while preserving these historical versions and their narrower claims.
